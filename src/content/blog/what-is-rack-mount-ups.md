A **rack mount UPS** is an uninterruptible power supply built to fit a standard **19-inch equipment rack**, the same cabinet that holds servers, network switches, storage and patch panels. Instead of a tower UPS standing on the floor beside the rack, the UPS bolts into the rack itself, takes up a few rack units, and powers the equipment around it.

Rack UPS are the usual choice for server rooms, telecom shelters, network closets and, increasingly, shop-floor panels where PLCs and controllers sit in an enclosed rack.

## Rack units: what 1U, 2U and 3U mean

Rack equipment is measured in **rack units (U)**. One U is 1.75 inches, or 44.45 mm, of vertical space. A typical full-height rack is 42U.

- **1U** (44.45 mm): very slim. Usually only small loads and short backup, because there is little room for batteries and cooling.
- **2U** (88.9 mm): the most common height for small rack UPS, with internal batteries for a few minutes at full load.
- **3U and above**: higher kVA ratings, more internal batteries, or a power module with its batteries in a separate unit.

The 19-inch figure is the width of the front mounting panel (482.6 mm). The usable opening between the rails is about 450 mm, so any 19-inch device fits any standard rack.

## Rack mount vs tower vs convertible

A **tower UPS** stands on the floor or a desk. A **rack mount UPS** sits on rails inside the cabinet. Many small UPS are sold as **tower/rack convertible** units: the same UPS can stand upright with feet, or lie flat in a rack with rail and ear kits.

Choose rack mount when the equipment is already in a rack, floor space is tight, or you want power, IT and cabling in one locked cabinet. Choose tower when there is no rack, or when the UPS and batteries are too large or heavy for the rack.

## Depth, weight and mounting

These are the details that cause installation problems most often.

### Depth

Rack UPS are deep, often deeper than the servers beside them. Check the UPS depth against the rack depth, and leave room behind it for power cables and airflow. Racks sold in India commonly come in 600 mm, 800 mm and 1000 mm depths; a shallow wall-mount network rack may not take a UPS at all.

### Weight

Batteries are heavy. A 2U or 3U UPS with internal batteries often weighs 20 kg or more, and battery modules add more. Mount the UPS and battery modules at the **bottom** of the rack, so the rack stays stable and nobody has to lift a heavy unit above shoulder height.

### Rails and support

Do not hang a heavy UPS from its front ears alone. Use the **rail kit** or a fixed shelf that supports it front and back. Four-post racks are needed for most UPS; two-post telecom frames suit only light units.

## External battery modules

Internal batteries in a rack UPS usually give only a few minutes at full load. For longer backup, add **external battery modules (EBMs)**: rack-mounted battery packs that plug into the UPS. Each one takes rack space and adds weight, so plan the U count for the UPS and all its modules together. To estimate how many you need, use the [UPS battery backup time guide](/guides/ups-battery-backup-time). Battery chemistry matters too; see [lithium vs lead-acid UPS batteries](/blogs/lithium-vs-lead-acid-ups-battery).

## Network cards and monitoring

A rack UPS usually powers networked equipment, so remote management matters. An **SNMP card** or adapter lets you watch the UPS from a browser or network management system, get alerts by email, log events and shut servers down gracefully before the battery runs out. ## What affects rack mount UPS price in India

Rack UPS prices vary widely, so a 1U or 2U price seen online may not match your actual need. The main cost drivers are:

1. **Capacity (kVA).** The biggest single factor.
2. **Topology.** A double conversion online UPS costs more than a line interactive one, but gives the load a fully regenerated, regulated sine wave. See [online vs offline UPS](/guides/online-vs-offline-ups).
3. **Backup time.** More minutes means more batteries and more external battery modules.
4. **Battery type.** Lithium usually costs more up front than VRLA but lasts longer and weighs less.
5. **Phases.** Three phase input or output costs more than single phase.
6. **Accessories.** Rail kits, network cards, monitoring software and an isolation transformer add to the price.
7. **Redundancy.** A second UPS or parallel module for N+1 adds cost but removes a single point of failure.

The surest way to compare prices is to fix the load in kVA, the backup time and the topology first. The [UPS sizing guide](/guides/ups-sizing-kva) helps with the load.
