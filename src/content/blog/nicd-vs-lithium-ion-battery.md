For industrial DC systems, **nickel cadmium (Ni-Cd)** wins on proven reliability, tolerance of heat, cold and abuse, and simple charging without electronics. **Lithium-ion**, usually lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) in stationary use, wins on space, weight, cycle life and built-in monitoring, but depends on a battery management system (BMS). Ni-Cd is the conservative choice for substations and power plants on float duty; lithium suits cycling duty and tight spaces.

If you are new to Ni-Cd, start with [what a nickel cadmium battery is](/blogs/batteries/nickel-cadmium-battery-explained). For lithium against lead-acid in a UPS, see [lithium vs lead-acid UPS batteries](/blogs/batteries/lithium-vs-lead-acid-ups-battery). This post compares the two premium options directly.

## Nickel cadmium vs lithium ion battery at a glance

| | Ni-Cd (pocket plate, vented) | Lithium-ion (LiFePO4) |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal cell voltage | 1.2 V | About 3.2 V |
| Cells for a 110 V system | Many, often 80 or more, set by the voltage window | Far fewer, set by the pack design |
| Float life | Very long, often 20 years or more | Long, typically well over a decade; depends on maker and duty |
| Cycle life | Good | Very good, often thousands of deep cycles |
| Temperature tolerance | Wide; works in heat and cold with limited loss of life | Good; charging in cold and life in sustained heat are limited by the cell maker |
| Electronics needed | None; works directly on a charger | BMS for cell balancing and protection |
| Failure mode | Gradual loss of capacity | Can be disconnected by its BMS on a fault |
| Weight and space | Large and heavy | Much smaller and lighter |
| Maintenance | Occasional distilled water top-up | Little routine maintenance; monitor BMS alarms |
| Monitoring | External | Built into the BMS |
| Upfront cost | High | High; depends on capacity and BMS |
| End of life | Cadmium is toxic; authorised recycler | Specialist lithium recycler |

The figures above are typical; always use the battery maker's data for your design.

## Where Ni-Cd still leads

### Reliability without electronics

A Ni-Cd bank is simply cells and links on a charger. Nothing in the battery can switch it off. In a substation, where the DC system must trip breakers during a fault, that simplicity is a major reason engineers stay with Ni-Cd.

### Heat, cold and abuse

Ni-Cd tolerates high and low temperatures, deep discharge, overcharge and long periods of neglect better than most chemistries. In hot control rooms without air-conditioning, or outdoor kiosks, it ages gracefully.

### Graceful ageing

As Ni-Cd ages, capacity falls slowly and predictably. Sudden open-circuit failure is rare, so a periodic capacity test is usually enough to plan replacement.

## Where lithium-ion leads

### Space and weight

A LiFePO4 bank of the same energy is far smaller and lighter, which matters for rooftop equipment rooms, retrofits into crowded panels, and mobile or rack-mounted systems.

### Cycling duty

Where the battery is discharged often, as in solar hybrid systems, sites with frequent outages or peak shaving, lithium's high cycle life and fast recharge are a real advantage.

### Built-in monitoring

The BMS measures cell voltages and temperatures and reports alarms, giving more visibility than a plain Ni-Cd bank.

## Safety and design points

- **Lithium chemistry matters.** LiFePO4 is far more thermally stable than the cobalt-based lithium chemistries used in phones and laptops, which is why it dominates stationary use. Still follow the maker's installation, ventilation and fire protection guidance and the applicable codes.
- **The BMS is part of the safety design.** Know what happens if the BMS disconnects the battery, and whether the DC system needs a second battery or charger for redundancy.
- **Ni-Cd vented cells release gas** on charge, so the room needs ventilation, as for flooded lead-acid.

## Charging is different

Ni-Cd and lithium need different chargers.

- **Ni-Cd** uses float and boost voltages set per cell, typically with a float cum boost charger (FCBC) and often a dropper to hold the load voltage within limits during boost. See [what an FCBC charger is](/blogs/batteries/fcbc-battery-charger-explained).
- **Lithium-ion** needs a constant current, constant voltage profile matched to its BMS, and usually communication between charger and BMS.

When switching chemistry on an existing DC system, check the charger, the DC voltage window and the protection settings, not just the battery.

## Which should you choose?

1. **Substation and power plant DC on float duty**, especially hot or remote sites: Ni-Cd is the established choice.
2. **Tight space or weight limits**, retrofits, rack systems: LiFePO4.
3. **Frequent cycling**, solar hybrid or unreliable mains: LiFePO4.
4. **Where the client specification names a chemistry**, follow it.
