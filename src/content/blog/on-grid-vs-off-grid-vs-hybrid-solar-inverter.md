Every solar plant needs an inverter to turn the DC from the panels into AC for your loads. The type of inverter decides whether the system can sell power to the grid, whether it keeps running in a power cut, and how much it costs. There are three types.

## The three types in one table

| | On-grid (grid-tie) | Off-grid | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connected to the grid | Yes | No (or grid as a charger only) | Yes |
| Batteries | No | Yes | Yes |
| Works in a power cut | No | Yes | Yes |
| Exports surplus to the grid | Yes, with net metering | No | Yes |
| Cost | Lowest | Medium | Highest |
| Best for | Cutting bills where power is reliable | Sites with no reliable grid | Cutting bills and backup |

## On-grid (grid-tie) inverters

A grid-tie inverter feeds solar power to your loads and exports any surplus to the grid. With net metering or net billing from your DISCOM, exported units offset what you draw at night, so the bill falls. Eligibility, capacity limits and tariffs differ by state and DISCOM, so check the current rules before sizing the plant.

There are no batteries, which keeps the cost down and the payback fast. The trade-off is important: **when the grid fails, a grid-tie inverter shuts down**, by design, so it does not push power into lines that may be under repair. Even on a sunny day, an on-grid system gives no backup.

## Off-grid inverters

An off-grid system stores solar power in batteries and runs the loads from them, with no connection to the grid, or with the grid or a generator only as a backup charger. It suits farms, remote sites and homes where the grid is absent or very unreliable.

Size the panels and batteries for your worst season, since there is no grid to fall back on. E&C's [off-grid solar inverter](/solar-off-grid-inverter), 1 to 5 kVA, has a built-in MPPT charge controller and battery equalisation, and works with mains or a generator as backup. For DC-only loads in rural homes, see the [solar DC UPS](/solar-dc-up).

## Hybrid inverters

A hybrid inverter combines both: it uses solar first, stores surplus in batteries, can export to the grid, and keeps your loads running from the batteries in a power cut. You set the priority between solar, battery and grid to suit your tariff and how often the power fails.

It costs more because of the batteries and the more capable inverter, but for businesses with regular power cuts it can replace a separate inverter for lights, fans and office loads while also cutting the bill. Most hybrid inverters take a few milliseconds to switch to battery, so they are not a zero-break supply like an online UPS.

## How to choose

1. **How often does the power fail?** Rarely: on-grid. Often: hybrid. No grid: off-grid.
2. **Is net metering available for your connection?** If yes, on-grid and hybrid systems can earn credit for surplus units.
3. **Which loads need backup?** Size the battery only for those loads, not the whole building. See [how to calculate battery backup time](/guides/ups-battery-backup-time).
4. **Do you run critical equipment?** Servers and process controls still need an [online UPS](/online-ups); solar reduces the energy bill, but a UPS gives the zero-break supply.

For battery-based systems built from separate parts, an [MPPT solar charge controller](/scc) harvests more energy from the panels than a basic PWM controller. E&C rates the gain at 25 to 30%; the real figure depends on panel voltage, temperature and battery state.
