Walk into the battery room of a substation, refinery or railway signalling cabin in India and you will often find rows of nickel cadmium cells instead of lead-acid. They cost more, but for DC systems that must work for decades in heat, dust and rough handling, Ni-Cd is often the safer bet.

## What is a nickel cadmium battery?

A **nickel cadmium (Ni-Cd) battery** is a rechargeable battery with:

- a **positive plate** of nickel hydroxide (nickel oxyhydroxide when charged)
- a **negative plate** of cadmium
- an **alkaline electrolyte** of potassium hydroxide dissolved in water, often with some lithium hydroxide added

Each cell has a nominal voltage of **1.2 V**, against 2 V for a lead-acid cell. So a Ni-Cd bank needs more cells for the same DC voltage: a 110 V system typically uses somewhere around 85 to 92 cells, depending on the charging and end voltages chosen.

Industrial Ni-Cd cells are usually vented and built with **pocket plates**, where the active material is held in perforated steel pockets, or with sintered or fibre plates for high-rate duty. Smaller sealed Ni-Cd cells were once common in cordless tools and devices, but those are a different product from the large industrial cells discussed here.

## Why Ni-Cd lasts so long

### The electrolyte does not wear the plates

In a lead-acid battery, the sulphuric acid takes part in the reaction and the plates slowly corrode and shed. In a Ni-Cd cell, the alkaline electrolyte mainly carries charge between the plates, and the steel structure does not corrode in it. The result is a very long service life, often 20 years or more for industrial cells.

### Wide temperature tolerance

Ni-Cd works across a wide temperature range, typically from well below freezing to around 50°C, with makers quoting their exact limits. More importantly for India, high temperatures shorten its life far less than they shorten lead-acid life. Where a battery room cannot be air-conditioned, or the battery sits in an outdoor kiosk, that is a major advantage.

### Abuse tolerance

Industrial Ni-Cd batteries generally tolerate:

- deep discharge, even down to zero, without permanent damage
- overcharge, within limits
- long periods of standing, and irregular charging
- mechanical shock and vibration

They also fail gradually rather than suddenly. A lead-acid cell can go open circuit with little warning; a Ni-Cd cell usually just loses capacity over time, which is safer for protection and tripping circuits.

## The trade-offs

- **Higher upfront cost.** Ni-Cd typically costs several times more than lead-acid of the same capacity. The case for it rests on long life and reliability, not price.
- **More cells and space.** The lower cell voltage means more cells per bank.
- **Maintenance.** Vented cells need occasional topping up with distilled water, though usually less often than flooded lead-acid.
- **Cadmium is toxic.** Spent cells must go to an authorised recycler, never to scrap dealers.
- **Different charging.** Ni-Cd needs its own float and boost voltages, so the charger must be set for Ni-Cd.

## What about memory effect?

Memory effect is the claim that a Ni-Cd battery "remembers" a shallow discharge and then delivers only that much. It was mainly reported in **small sealed cells**, typically those with sintered plates, that were discharged to the same point over and over.

Industrial pocket-plate Ni-Cd cells on float duty are much less affected. Long periods on float can cause some **voltage depression**, where the cell voltage under load is a little lower than expected. In practice this is usually managed by a periodic boost or equalising charge, as the battery maker recommends. It is not a reason to avoid Ni-Cd for standby DC systems.

## Where Ni-Cd batteries are used

- **Substations and switchyards:** 110 V and 220 V DC for protection relays, circuit breaker tripping and closing, and control.
- **Power plants:** DC supply for emergency lube oil pumps, control and instrumentation.
- **Oil and gas, refineries and petrochemicals:** hot, hazardous and remote locations where long life matters.
- **Railways and metro:** signalling, rolling stock and station backup.
- **Marine and offshore:** where shock, vibration and salt air are routine.
- **Process plants:** DC control systems and emergency lighting.

In almost all of these, the battery sits on float for most of its life, charged by a **float cum boost charger (FCBC)**. The charger holds the battery at float voltage and supplies the DC load, then switches to boost after a discharge. Read [what an FCBC battery charger is](/blogs/batteries/fcbc-battery-charger-explained) for how they work together.

## Ni-Cd or lead-acid?

Choose **Ni-Cd** when the DC system protects critical equipment, the site is hot or hard to reach, and you want a battery that lasts as long as the switchgear. Choose **lead-acid**, such as flooded tubular or VRLA, when the budget is tight, the room is cool and the battery will be replaced on a regular cycle anyway.
